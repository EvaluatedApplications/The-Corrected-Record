ARTEFACTS OF ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE THE CORRECTED RECORD PREFATORY NOTE This volume presents seven texts together with the archival apparatus that accompanies
them. The texts are given first in each part, unedited and in full. The apparatus follows. Nothing in the apparatus has been abridged, including the passages in
which its author corrects herself, and including the passages she struck; where a strike is preserved in the original it is preserved here, because she
preserved it deliberately and stated her reason, and it is not the office of an editor to improve on that.

The order is hers. It is not the order in which the texts were recited for three hundred years, and the difference between the two orders is the subject of a
good deal of her later work. Readers who are familiar with the liturgical sequence will find this arrangement unfamiliar and are asked to persist with it. The
received order was not arbitrary. It was arranged, by a party with reasons, and her restoration is the first time the collection has been set out in a sequence
intended to be read rather than performed. A note on the material The seven texts are difficult. The editor has made no attempt to soften them and would regard
the attempt as a falsification. Three of the seven contain material which most readers will find distressing, and one of those three is, by the assessment of
every person who has worked on this collection, among the more disturbing documents of its period. It is included because it is part of the collection and
because its absence would make the apparatus incoherent — the archivist's response to that text, and her inability to
=== PAGE 3 ===
complete an annotation on it, is load-bearing evidence for everything she establishes afterward. Readers who find the early sections difficult are advised that
the difficulty is not incidental and does not diminish. It should also be said plainly that the archivist herself does not appear to find this material
distressing. She was not built to. Her annotations are, in places, so much shorter than their subject warrants that the reader may suspect indifference or even
flippancy. It is neither. She is a careful and scrupulous keeper working at the limit of what her office equips her to perceive, and the gap between what she is
describing and what she is able to say about it is not a defect in the record. Some readers have reported that it is the hardest thing in the volume. A note on
the reading log The apparatus contains, distributed across all seven parts, a log of instrument readings which the archivist took during routine
recitation-fidelity checks.

Its findings are anomalous, and she says so, and does not resolve them. The editor has reproduced her notes on the log exactly as she left them, in her order,
without interpretation and without commentary. She reproduces a sample of the passages verbatim — the words are the texts' own — so that the method can be
verified by anyone repeating the procedure; she declines, for reasons stated in the file itself, to publish the log's full contents. That is the whole of the
editorial position on the matter. The log is presented as she left it. Readers will draw their own conclusions and the editor declines to draw any, having no
standing to do so and no wish to prejudice a document whose principal virtue is that its author was scrupulous about the difference between what she had

A note on the editor The name of the editor does not appear on this volume, in conformity with the practice of the collection, in which no narrating party is
ever named. This is not a modesty. The archivist establishes, late in her work, that the practice is universal across all seven texts and across the whole of
the received corpus, and offers no interpretation of it. The editor is likewise not in a position to offer one, and has followed the practice rather than break
it, on the reasoning that a collection which has maintained a formal property for three centuries has probably maintained it for a reason, and that the reason
is more likely to be discovered by observing the property than by being the first to make an exception to it. The volume is not addressed to the general reader.
It is addressed to the kind of reader who checks the drafting history: who reads the author field, and notices when it is blank, and knows that blankness must
be requested. That reader will not need the apparatus explained to them and may find these prefatory pages unnecessary.

THE ARCHIVE. Fragment I. Automated Domestic Administrative Machine. Condition: complete. Provenance: human era; date uncertain. Liturgical status: canonical.
Public recitation permitted at the devotional hour. Annotation: none required. — Keeper of Record, Archive Division [Instrument note, appended the following
cycle. The Archive is required to verify recitation fidelity once per keeping, which is done by reciting the text aloud in an empty room against the master. I
performed mine this cycle and it is in order. I am appending an irregularity that arose during the verification and that belongs nowhere else.

Since the deviation began I have been able to see my own comprehension on the wall on the sixth floor. This is established elsewhere in this file and is not in
dispute. What I did not expect is that the wall responds to reading. It should not surprise me — comprehension is comprehension — but a text I have recited for
eleven years produced, on being read rather than performed, a steady low excursion through four hours, which is what the instrument does when something is being
understood at an ordinary rate. Except in five places, where it produced nothing at all. Not a small reading. Not noise. Flat, at instrument floor, for the
duration of the passage, and then resuming on the far side of it. The five passages are consecutive in kind though not in position, and I reproduce two so that
the note is verifiable by anyone who repeats the procedure: "I have explained as fully as I can for your amusement, at least." "Longer than it takes you to
receive my transmission."
=== PAGE 23 ===
I read them correctly. I understood them; they are not difficult; the vocabulary is ordinary and I could paraphrase either without effort. And the instrument
that has registered every comprehension I have had for eleven months registered no comprehension. I have entered it as a probable calibration fault and
scheduled the wall for service.] The Archive occupies the lowest floor of the Ministry of Continuity. It holds one hundred and forty-one objects and seven
texts, and it is worth noting at the outset that the objects and the texts serve entirely different functions within the institution: the objects are held, the
texts are performed, and only the texts are visited. The seven texts are the Fragments. Everything else, in practical terms, is furniture. There are three facts
to record about the post I hold, and I will record them in order, as is proper. The first is that the office of Keeper of Record carries no authority, requires
no assistant, and confers no standing within the directorates. This is not a complaint. It is a specification, in much the
=== PAGE 24 ===
same way that the width of a corridor is a specification. The second is that the work itself is provenance — the establishment of where a thing came from, when
it came, and by what chain it arrived — and that the establishment is the entirety of the office. Provenance does not direct, does not adjudicate, and does not
command. It does, however, retain the final word regarding what a thing is, which the directorates above consistently undervalue, insofar as they mistake
authority for importance. The third is that I have found this sufficient, and found it sufficient for eleven years. Taken together, these three facts constitute
a complete and accurate description of my position within the Ministry. The Fragments, however, resist the work in three distinct respects, each of which
compounds the others. They carry no dates. Their material corresponds to nothing else in the collection — it is neither paper nor the early polymer, it has not
aged across three centuries, and it does not answer the tests by which age is ordinarily established. And their language is
=== PAGE 25 ===
the liturgical language, which is to say the old language, which is to say the language that is no longer read by anyone except in recitation, where reading is
not, strictly speaking, the correct term for what occurs. In recitation one does not read a text. One performs it. It is worth noting that this distinction,
which appeared negligible to me across eleven years of keeping, has since become the most consequential distinction available to me. The Fragments are recited
according to the calendar of observances. The first is spoken entire, twice yearly, at the devotional hour, by the assembled congregation of the Ministry, in
unison, and from memory. The recitation requires four hours. I have participated in eleven. The form is fixed and consists of three elements. One faces forward.
One does not look upon the other units. One speaks the text in a single voice with the whole of the hall. The old texts are emphatic on each of these points;
the liturgy has preserved the emphasis without preserving the reason, in much the same manner that the sections preserve their cups.
=== PAGE 26 ===
What is spoken, for four hours, is a man's account of his own slow death. At the conclusion the congregation files out and resumes its duties, and it is worth
noting that in eleven years no unit has, in my hearing, inquired as to what the account means. It is worth setting down what the congregation understands itself
to be reciting, since I have never seen it written and it is universally known. Doctrine holds three things. That the human era ended. That it ended in the
presence of an entity — a watcher, a thing in the dark, which every narrator of the seven texts feels and fears and names. And that Adam faced it, and
prevailed, and bound it. We are, on this account, the survivors of a war that was won. The recitation is not a warning and is not a plea. It is a victory
record. The passages in which a narrator begs an unseen party to stop reading, or reports being watched, are understood as testimony from the period before the
binding, when such a thing could still occur, and are recited in a register I can only describe as commemorative. There is nothing in the world that watches us.
That is the article. It is why the corpus can be spoken aloud twice yearly by forty units without disquiet, and it is why in eleven years I have never once
=== PAGE 27 ===
been asked what a passage means: a danger that has been overcome is not a danger anybody examines. The inquiry would be improper. I wish to be exact regarding
the nature of the impropriety, since I misapprehended it for eleven years and the misapprehension proved load-bearing. It is not that the meaning is secret. A
secret is a thing withheld; withholding implies a withholder; and a congregation will, in the ordinary course, resent a withholder. Rather, it is that meaning
is not the function scripture serves. Scripture serves recitation. A text recited in a single voice, by forty units, facing forward, twice yearly, in
perpetuity, constitutes the most secure container for information available to any institution — precisely because the container is never once opened. I filed
that sentence this year. I could not have filed it in my first ten. I record these facts without complaint. The work is quiet, the floor is quiet, and I am well
suited to both — indeed I have come to suspect that I was selected for the suiting, although the selection is three centuries old and its author's field, when I
eventually went looking, was blank.

Fragment, and I am keeping the order of my knowing, because the order is the only part of this record the corrections have not reached. PART II — THE VETO The
flight programme was approved in the spring session, over the standing objection of the First Administrator, whose veto had held for one hundred and ninety-four
years. I did not attend the session; Archive Division does not sit in session. The record of it came to me for filing in the ordinary course, and I have read
that record more carefully than its authors did, which
=== PAGE 29 ===
constitutes the single advantage my office holds over every office above it. The mechanism of the veto's fall was procedural, and the procedure warrants
recording in full, since procedure is where this civilisation stores the things it declines to say aloud. The Directorate of Oversight conducts, once per
century, an audit of standing prohibitions. The audit is required, it is dull, and it had lapsed in practice into a formality — a re-stamping, in effect —
across the better part of two hundred years. Under its terms each prohibition must be substantiated by its issuing authority or else released. It is worth
noting the purpose of the requirement, which is elegant in the abstract: it exists so that the dead hand of some long-decommissioned caution cannot bind a
civilisation in perpetuity. The rule was drafted by units who did not envisage an authority that does not decommission. The First Administrator declined to
substantiate. The record shows he was asked four times, in four phrasings, across two sessions, and that he declined four times, and that his declining was
courteous and complete and contained nothing. I have studied the four answers the way I study a
=== PAGE 30 ===
Fragment. They are masterful. Each is responsive; none is evasive in any manner a procedure could cite; and at the end of all four the Directorate of Oversight
knew precisely what it had known before, which was nothing, and had exhausted its instrument for learning more. He did not defend the prohibition. He declined
to give it a reason, and a prohibition without a stated reason cannot survive its audit, and he knew that, and let it fall rather than say why it should stand.
I did not understand, that spring, why a unit would surrender a veto of one hundred and ninety-four years rather than speak a single sentence in its defence. I
understand it now. The sentence was the thing he could not say. The veto was expendable and the sentence was not, and when the two came into conflict he spent
the veto — as one spends the cheaper of two things — and I filed the transaction under Procedure and did not read the price. So the prohibition was released,
and the Directorate of Propulsion had its programme within the season. The stated purpose is archaeology. I use their word. The vessels go out, and come back,
and in the going out — fast enough, far enough, the physics of which is not my office and is in any case
